Grammarly Free vs Pro in 2026: Is $12/Month Actually Worth It?

Updated 15 November 2026 · 7 min read · AI for Work HQ Editorial

Short answer

  • Grammarly Free is enough if you write emails, messages and occasional documents.
  • Pro pays for itself if you write for clients, publish regularly, or need plagiarism and AI-detection checks.
  • The price trap: $12/month is the annual rate. You pay $144 upfront. Monthly billing is $30/month — $360 a year.
  • Break-even: annual beats monthly after about 5 months. Don't commit for a year to save money you'd lose in three months.

Grammarly is one of those tools almost everyone installs and almost nobody thinks about. The free version is good enough that people forget a paid tier exists — until they hit a suggestion that says "upgrade to see this rewrite."

So here's the honest breakdown: what free actually covers, what Pro unlocks, and the point where the subscription stops being a nice-to-have.

What Free actually gives you

More than most people assume:

  • Spelling, grammar and punctuation correction
  • Basic tone detection (it tells you if you sound confident, formal, etc.)
  • Clarity suggestions on awkward sentences
  • 100 AI prompts per month
  • Works in the browser, desktop app, Word, and most web editors

For a freelancer writing emails, proposals and the odd client update, that covers roughly 90% of the value. The corrections are the part people actually need, and they're free.

What Pro unlocks

  • Full-sentence rewrites — Free shows you the suggestion exists, Pro shows the rewritten sentence
  • Tone adjustment — rewrite a blunt email as a diplomatic one without rewriting it yourself
  • Plagiarism detection — checks against billions of web pages
  • AI-text detection — flags whether a passage reads as machine-generated
  • 2,000 AI prompts per month instead of 100
  • Team features — style guides, brand tones, shared snippets (irrelevant if you're solo)

Notice what's not on that list: better grammar checking. The grammar engine is the same. Pro sells you rewriting, verification and volume.

The billing maths that matters

This is where most people lose money.

The annual plan saves $216 a year versus monthly. Put differently: monthly billing costs 2.5× the annual price for the exact same product.

But annual is charged $144 upfront, not $12 a month. If you're testing whether Pro fits your workflow, that's a lot of money to gamble on a guess. The sensible sequence is:

  1. Use the 10-day trial (usually offered at signup)
  2. If you're still unsure, pay for one month at $30
  3. Only go annual once you've used it in a real week of work

The break-even between monthly and annual is about 4.8 months. If you're confident you'll still be using it in six months, annual is the right call. If not, monthly is cheaper in practice, not just in theory.

Where Grammarly is the wrong tool

Worth saying plainly: Grammarly fixes writing you've already done. It doesn't produce the first draft.

If your bottleneck is staring at a blank page — client proposals, blog posts, cover letters — a general AI assistant will help more per dollar. ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro at $20/month generate the draft; Grammarly polishes it. Different jobs.

If you're deciding between a writing assistant and a paraphraser, that's a separate comparison: Grammarly vs QuillBot.

Verdict

Start free. Upgrade the month you start writing for clients. Not before.

The free plan is genuinely useful and doesn't feel like a demo. Pro earns its $12 when a rewritten sentence saves you ten minutes of editing, when a plagiarism check saves you an embarrassing mistake, or when English isn't your first language and you're selling into an English market. Outside those cases, $144 a year is better spent on a tool that does something Grammarly can't.
